**The Swimming Carnival**

Priya could swim. That was not the problem. The problem was the whole school watching.

She had told nobody about the knot in her stomach — not her mum, not even her best friend Tess, who had already won two ribbons and was bouncing on her toes beside the pool.

"You'll be fine," Tess said, reading her face anyway. "Just look at the black line on the bottom. Not the crowd. The line."

The starter raised his arm. Priya's heart thudded against her ribs. Then — *beep!* — and she was in.

The water closed over her like a door shutting out the world. There was the black line, steady and patient. She followed it. One arm, then the other. Breathe. One arm, then the other. Breathe.

She did not know she had finished until her hand smacked the wall and the noise came rushing back — shouting, splashing, whistles. She lifted her goggles.

Fourth place. Not a ribbon. Not even close to Tess's time.

But Priya was laughing, coughing, hauling herself out of the pool with arms that felt like jelly, because for fifty metres she had forgotten to be afraid, and that was a thing she had never done before.

"See?" said Tess, throwing a towel at her head. "Told you."

## Text B — Information Report（8题）

**The Frilled-neck Lizard**

The frilled-neck lizard is one of Australia's most extraordinary reptiles. It lives in the woodlands of northern Australia, where it spends most of its day clinging to tree trunks.

*The frill*
Around its neck, this lizard has a large flap of loose skin called a frill. Most of the time the frill lies folded flat against its shoulders. But when the lizard is frightened, it opens its mouth wide and the frill springs out like an umbrella — up to 25 centimetres across. At the same time, the lizard hisses, stands on its back legs and lashes its tail. To a predator, a small lizard suddenly looks like a much bigger, angrier animal.

*Running away*
If bluffing does not work, the frilled-neck lizard has a second trick. It sprints away on its two back legs, holding its front legs and tail in the air. Because of this, it is sometimes nicknamed the "bicycle lizard".

*Food and life*
Frilled-neck lizards eat insects — mostly ants, beetles and termites. They can grow up to 90 centimetres long, though about two-thirds of that length is tail. In the dry season, when food is scarce, they become much less active and may not eat for weeks.

## Text E — Procedure / 说明（8题）

**Making a Bird Feeder from a Milk Bottle**

Australian birds visit gardens all year round. You can make them a feeder in about twenty minutes.

*You will need:*
- a clean 2-litre plastic milk bottle with lid
- scissors (ask an adult to help)
- a wooden spoon or stick
- string
- birdseed

*What to do:*
1. Wash the bottle well and let it dry completely. **Any milk left inside will go sour and could make birds sick.**
2. Ask an adult to cut two small holes opposite each other, about 8 cm up from the base.
3. Push the wooden spoon through both holes so the handle sticks out. This is the perch where birds will stand.
4. Just above the perch, cut a hole about the size of a 20c coin. This is where birds will reach the seed.
5. Cut two small slits near the neck of the bottle and thread the string through. Tie the ends together.
6. Pour birdseed into the bottle until it reaches just below the feeding hole.
7. Hang the feeder from a branch, at least 2 metres above the ground and away from fences where cats can climb.

*Looking after your feeder:* Empty and wash the feeder every two weeks. Wet seed grows mould, which is dangerous for birds.
